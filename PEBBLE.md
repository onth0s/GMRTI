# PEBBLE: The Plain-English Alignment Guide

## 1. Introduction: The Loop of Language

If you look up a word in a dictionary, it defines it using other words. If you
look up those words, they point to even more words. Eventually, you will loop
back to where you started. Language has no "ground floor." Because definitions
are circular, we can never define anything with absolute mathematical precision.

Yet, we communicate every day. When you buy a coffee, you and the barista agree
on the transaction without exchanging internal dictionaries. How? We make
appropriate noises at appropriate times ("hacemos ruidos convenientemente"). We
do not share identical concepts; we simply have maps that are close enough to
let us cooperate. This is called a **Comosí** (an "As-If" agreement). We act *as
if* we understand each other.

This guide distills the General Method for Refinement and Transmission of Ideas
(GMRTI) into a low-overhead, jargon-free framework. It is designed to help you
align your mental map with another person—or with your future self.

---

## 2. The Philosophy: Doubt and Drift

### The Illusion of Agreement
A Comosí has a limited range. It works perfectly in one setting but shatters in
another.
*Example*: Two people in a house agree on which objects are "paintings" and
which are "sculptures." Their personal dictionaries seem identical. Later, they
visit a modern art gallery. Faced with avant-garde art, their agreement breaks.
Why? Because a painting with sufficient embossing can step into the sculpture
category. Their dictionaries never assigned a hard boundary to the distinction;
the new context simply exposed the hidden differences.

### The Moving Target (Temporal Drift)
You are not a static observer. Your mind is a collection of experiences that
constantly changes. A note you write to yourself today is written by one
"semantic entity" — the particular you that exists at this moment, with this
vocabulary and these concerns. The person reading it in five years is a
"temporal stranger" — someone who shares your name and memories but not your
current understanding. Without active anchoring, you will misread your own past
thoughts because your internal dictionary has drifted.

## 3. The Core Concept: Connections Over Labels

Traditional thinking assumes words are stable boxes of meaning. GMRTI assumes
the opposite:
1. **Nodes are Emergent**: A concept (a word or node) has no meaning in isolation.
   It is merely a junction where multiple relationships meet.
2. **Edges are Primary**: The meaning lives entirely in the *connections* (edges)
   between concepts. If you change the connections, you change the concept.
3. **Suspended Charity (The Mirror)**: To test a map, you must suspend your
   natural tendency to "fill in the blanks" for the author. You must read the
   map with zero charity, using only the explicitly declared connections.

## 4. Bootstrapping: How to Start from Zero

If we start with as few shared assumptions as possible, how can we begin to
cooperate? (The substrate below is the minimum non-negotiable floor — not a void,
but the smallest set of capacities any communicating agent must already possess.)
We divide our shared understanding into two layers:

### The Minimal Substrate
This is the framework-independent toolkit that any conscious agent shares. It is the
ability to:
*   Distinguish a structured signal from random noise.
*   Detect structural similarity between patterns.
*   Recognize the boundaries of our own conceptual regions.

### The Primitive Covenant
This is the GMRTI-specific agreement to use the **8 Basic Connections** and the
**Tier 1 Coarse Taxonomy** — a first-pass sorting of links into three broad
kinds (inferential, causal, or other) that gets you orienting quickly before
finer distinctions are needed.

### The Covenant Zero Protocol (CZP)
How do we build the Primitive Covenant out of the Minimal Substrate? We play simple
association games:
*   **Mapping Causality**: We pair physical signals with temporal co-occurrence
    (e.g., "When button A is pressed, light B turns on") to define `[->causal]`.
*   **Mapping Inference**: We verify logical patterns (e.g., "If shape A is present,
    shape B is always present") to define `[->inferential]`.
*   **Mapping Identity**: We establish signal substitution (e.g., swapping symbol A
    for symbol B without changing the result) to define `[->identity]`.

Once these games establish our primary link types, our coarse taxonomy is active.

## 5. The Eight Basic Connections

To build a map, you must explicitly declare how your concepts connect using these
eight relationship types:

1.  **Causal** (`[->causal]`): Concept A physically makes Concept B happen.
    *Example*: *Fire* `[->causal]` *Smoke*.
2.  **Inferential** (`[->inferential]`): If Concept A is true, Concept B must also be true.
    *Example*: *Socrates is human* `[->inferential]` *Socrates is mortal*.
3.  **Procedural** (`[->procedural]`): You must do Concept A before doing Concept B.
    *Example*: *Pour batter* `[->procedural]` *Bake cake*.
4.  **Normative** (`[->normative]`): Concept A justifies or obligates Concept B.
    *Example*: *Promise made* `[->normative]` *Keep promise*.
5.  **Identity** (`[->identity]`): Concept A is semantically equivalent to Concept B.
    *Example*: *Water* `[->identity]` *H2O*.
6.  **Referential** (`[->referential]`): Concept A points to a real-world object or instance B.
    *Example*: *The Word "Chair"* `[->referential]` *The physical chair in the corner*.
7.  **Affective** (`[->affective]`): Concept A drives or motivates Concept B — the
    subjective pull, experienced from the inside.
    *Example*: *Fear* `[->affective]` *Flight*.
    (Causal links describe mechanism from the outside — the physical chain,
    independent of anyone's experience of it. Fear can also be Causal when the
    neurological pathway is the link being asserted. Use Affective when the
    claim is about motivation; use Causal when the claim is about mechanism.)
8.  **Structural** (`[->structural]`): Concept A is part of the physical or logical makeup of Concept B.
    *Example*: *Wheel* `[->structural]` *Bicycle*.

## 6. Testing and Repairing the Web

Once you have drawn your map, you must maintain it. GMRTI uses four diagnostic
mechanisms to verify and repair maps:

### Path-Stripping Probing (PSP)
When you explain a concept, you often provide multiple ways to reach the same conclusion
(redundant paths). A receiver might have a misunderstanding (a void) in one path but still
reach the correct conclusion by taking a backup route.
*   **The Diagnostic**: To catch these hidden voids, temporarily disable all but one
    path. If the receiver can still reconstruct the meaning, the path is sound.
*   **Polyphonic Node Pre-screening**: PSP can cause false alarms on complex nodes that
    legitimately carry multiple independent meanings. A node is "polyphonic" when
    several genuinely different kinds of connections come in and go out — think of a
    word like "bank," linking to money, rivers, and airplane maneuvers all at once.
    Pre-screen your map and flag these busy nodes. On them, PSP only declares a void
    if *every single* path through the node fails, not just one.

### Proactive Chaotic Probing (PCP)
Do not wait for communication to fail during a crisis. During quiet times, run PSP on
randomly selected nodes in your documentation. Simulating failures forces latent
misunderstandings to the surface.

### The Null-Effect Equivalence Axiom
If you and another person have different internal definitions for a concept, but that
difference has absolutely no effect on your actions or conclusions across all scenarios,
the difference is semantically null. Do not waste energy trying to align it.

### Boundary-Tracing Encapsulation (BTE)
When you fix a misunderstanding, you alter a connection. Because concepts are interconnected,
changing one link can cause a domino effect that shifts the meaning of unrelated concepts
across your entire map.
*   **The Wrapper**: Wrap the corrected area in a boundary covenant. Treat it as a
    "black box" with a strictly declared set of inputs and outputs.
*   **The Warning (Holistic Leakage)**: BTE provides practical isolation, not absolute
    theoretical isolation. If the context outside the boundary changes, it can still
    indirectly alter the meaning of the boundary's interface connections. Keep your
    interface sets small and explicitly typed.

### Just-In-Time Ontological Marking (JIT-OM)
Do not try to define and categorize every single concept in advance. Only apply formal
labels (like our 8 connection types) when a real misunderstanding occurs. Use the simplest
level of detail necessary to resolve the immediate conflict.

## 7. Content vs. Protocol: Avoiding the Meta-Loop

When trying to resolve a misunderstanding, it is easy to get trapped in an endless loop
of "talking about talking." To prevent this, GMRTI stratifies all communication into two
separate layers:

1.  **The Content Layer**: This is the map itself. It uses our 8 relational operators
    to discuss the actual topic (e.g., software design, philosophical agreements).
2.  **The Protocol Layer**: This is a closed vocabulary of exactly 6 commands used
    strictly to coordinate the discussion:
    *   `propose`: Suggest a specific connection type or repair.
    *   `accept`: Agree to the proposed change.
    *   `reject`: Disagree with the proposed change.
    *   `pause`: Temporarily halt transmission to run a diagnostic.
    *   `resume`: Continue transmission after a diagnostic or repair.
    *   `default`: Fall back to the highest-risk calibration (Audit Mode) when agreement fails.

No protocol command may be used as a content concept, and no content concept may alter
the protocol rules. This single boundary prevents infinite regress.

## 8. Anchoring Across Time

Since semantic covenants drift, how do we keep our long-term guides stable? GMRTI treats
written documentation as an **Artifact Anchor** that we must actively maintain.

### The Damping Factor Axiom
When updating a written guide based on a new experience, do not rewrite the entire guide
to fit the new context. Make small, controlled adjustments. All updates are scaled by a
damping factor λ, a number less than 1 (typically 0.15). Nudge the guide a little toward
the new proposal — don't overwrite it.[^1]

[^1]: The precise formula is: Updated Guide = Current Guide + λ (New Proposal − Current Guide). This is the only formal notation in the document; the prose description above captures the same idea without requiring algebraic fluency.

This prevents the guide from oscillating wildly between different viewpoints.

### The Convergence Theorem
If the sum of all changes you propose to a guide over time is finite, the guide is
mathematically guaranteed to converge to a stable, permanent coordinate. It will not
drift indefinitely. (The damping factor reduces the size of each update, but the
theorem requires the additional condition that successive proposals themselves
diminish or stabilize — the damping rule alone does not by itself guarantee a
finite sum.)

## 9. The 3-Step Alignment Checklist

Use this checklist whenever you write a critical note to your future self or need to
align on a complex task with someone else:

1.  **Map the Key Concepts**: Draw a simple network of the key terms. Explicitly label the
    links using the 8 Basic Connections.
2.  **Run the Agnostic Mirror**: Read the map (or have your partner read it) with zero charity.
    Pretend you know nothing about the topic except what is written on the page. Can you still
    reach the correct conclusion?
3.  **Encapsulate the Repairs**: If you find a gap, correct the specific link, wrap the corrected
    concepts in a BTE boundary, and declare its interface.

## Coda: The Pebble and the Boulder

Sisyphus got a boulder — heavy, immovable, an eternal punishment. He pushes it up
the hill, it rolls back down, he pushes it again. The myth is about futility: a
task that never completes, a surface that never yields.

This document gives you a pebble instead.

Small enough to hold. Light enough to shape. But sharp enough to grind against
your shoe until you name the friction. The whole apparatus — the covenants, the
probing, the damping — is what you do with the pebble instead of pushing it up
a hill forever.

The pebble was never the problem. The un-named friction it was making against
the shoe was.
